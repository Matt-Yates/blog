Top 5 beginner to intermediate woodworking projects

#1

Cutting Board

Beginner

The foundational woodworking project. Edge-grain construction teaches panel gluing, squaring, and food-safe finishing. Make one for yourself, gift one to someone you love.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 4× "× 3⅝" × 18" — Maple (5 strips)
  • 4× "× 3⅝" × 18" — Walnut (4 strips, alternating)
Materials
  • Wood glue (1 bottle)
  • Clamps (4+ bar clamps or pipe clamps)
  • Food-safe mineral oil (16 oz)
  • 220-grit sandpaper

Steps

  1. Cut strips to width. Rip maple and walnut boards to 3⅝" wide on the table saw. You need 9 strips total — 5 maple, 4 walnut.
  2. Arrange in pattern. Lay strips face-up, alternating maple and walnut. Flip every other strip end-for-end to hide end-grain pattern. Add a scrap spacer on each end.
  3. Glue the panel. Apply thin glue to each joint, clamp with spreaders. Wipe squeeze-out with a damp cloth. Let dry 24 hours.
  4. Cut to final size. Trim to 12" × 18". Check square with diagonal measurement. Both diagonals must match — if they don't, it's out of square.
  5. Sand the surface. Progress through 120 → 180 → 220 grit. Sand with the grain. The board should feel like glass at 220.
  6. Round the edges. Use a ⅛" round-over bit on the router table or sand edges by hand. This prevents splinters and gives a finished look.
  7. Apply finish. Coat generously with food-safe mineral oil. Let soak 30 minutes, wipe excess. Repeat 3 times over 3 days. This is what makes it food-safe.
Tools: Table saw, clamps, router (optional), sandpaper, mineral oil
#2

Floating Shelves

Beginner

Minimal materials, maximum visual impact. A hidden-bracket shelf teaches precision cutting and wall-mount hardware. Build one to start — then make a set of three for a gallery wall.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 1× 1×10 × 36" — Pine, Oak, or Poplar
  • 1× 1×10 × 34" — Same species (shelf face)
  • 2× 1×4 × 8" — L-bracket arms
  • 2× M6×40mm L-brackets (2-pack)
Materials
  • Wood glue
  • 1¼" wood screws (6)
  • Wall anchors (2, if studs don't align)
  • Spray stain or polyurethane
  • 220-grit sandpaper

Steps

  1. Cut the shelf face. Rip the 1×10 to 8⅞" wide. This fits inside a standard L-bracket (which has an 8" width) with a tiny gap for expansion.
  2. Cut the L-bracket arms. Cut two 1×4 pieces to 10" long. These slide behind the shelf face to create the "floating" illusion.
  3. Glue and clamp the shelf face to the brackets. Center the bracket arms on the back edge of the shelf face. Apply glue, clamp, let dry 2 hours.
  4. Sand and finish. Sand all surfaces to 220 grit. Apply stain or clear coat. This is the step where the shelf starts looking like furniture.
  5. Find wall studs. Use a stud finder. Mark center of each stud. If your bracket holes don't align with studs, use wall anchors rated for 50+ lbs each.
  6. Mount brackets to wall. Hold shelf against wall at desired height. Mark screw holes through bracket. Pre-drill, then drive screws. Use a level — this is critical.
Tools: Table saw or circular saw, drill, level, clamps, stud finder
#3

Planter Box

Beginner

The forgiving first build. A simple box teaches assembly, angles, and material selection. Mistakes are hidden by plants. Build a set — one for herbs, one for flowers, one for each room.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 2× 1×6 × 14" — Front & Back panels
  • 2× 1×6 × 12" — Side panels
  • 1× 1×6 × 14" — Bottom panel (cut from scrap)
  • 2× 1×4 × 14" — Drainage rim (optional)
Materials
  • Exterior wood glue
  • 2" exterior screws (12)
  • Landscaping fabric (1 ft²)
  • Drill bit ¼" (for drainage holes)
  • Container soil + mulch

Steps

  1. Cut all panels. Front/back at 14", sides at 12". The sides are shorter because they fit inside the front and back — that's how the box gets its depth.
  2. Drill drainage holes. Drill four ¼" holes in the bottom panel, spaced evenly. This is non-negotiable — plants rot without drainage.
  3. Assemble the box. Apply glue to each joint. Drive screws through the front panel into the sides. Repeat for back panel. Use clamps to keep everything square.
  4. Nail on the bottom. Slide the bottom panel into place under the side panels. Screw up through the bottom into the sides. This creates a solid base.
  5. Line with fabric. Cut landscaping fabric to fit inside the bottom. Staple it in place. This holds soil in while letting water drain.
  6. Fill and plant. Add soil, plant your herbs or flowers, and enjoy. Cedar or redwood last longest outdoors — pine works for a season or two.
Tools: Miter saw or table saw, drill, 2" screws, clamps, staple gun (optional)
#4

Side Table

Intermediate

The classic first furniture piece. Bridges small crafts and real furniture. Teaches leg joinery, square assembly, and finishing — skills that unlock every cabinet and table after.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 4× 1×4 × 22" — Legs
  • 2× 1×4 × 14" — Side rails (lower cross supports)
  • 1× 1×10 × 24" — Table top
Materials
  • Wood glue
  • 1½" pocket hole screws (16)
  • 2" wood screws (4)
  • Wood stain or polyurethane
  • 120 → 220 grit sandpaper

Steps

  1. Drill pocket holes. On each end of the two side rails, drill 4 pocket holes — one near each end. This is where the rails attach to the legs.
  2. Build two leg assemblies. Attach each side rail between two legs using pocket holes. Glue + screws. Clamp square. Repeat for the other side. Each side is a "U" shape.
  3. Connect the sides. Attach the two leg assemblies together using the remaining 1×4 as a front and back rail (same 14" length). Pocket holes through legs into these rails.
  4. Flip and attach top. Flip the frame upside down. Center the table top on it. Screw down through the frame into the top from underneath. Use 2" screws.
  5. Sand everything. Sand the top surface from 120 to 220 grit. Sand the frame edges to remove saw marks. A sander saves time; a sanding block gives control.
  6. Finish. Apply stain if desired. Let dry 4 hours. Apply polyurethane (2 coats, sanding between coats). This protects the top from water rings and heat marks.
Tools: Table saw, drill, pocket hole jig, clamps, sander, polyurethane
#5

Sawhorses

Beginner

Build the thing that lets you build everything else. Two sawhorses + a plywood top = a workbench that costs under $30. Simple construction, massive practical value. Built in an afternoon.

Build Plan

Cut List (per sawhorse)
  • 2× 2×4 × 36" — Legs (cut to angle)
  • 1× 2×4 × 28" — Top beam
  • 1× 2×4 × 24" — Cross brace
  • 2× 2×4 × 28" — Side blocks (for stability)
Materials (×2 sawhorses)
  • 3× 2×4 × 8ft (one board = both sawhorses)
  • 3" structural screws (24)
  • Wood glue
  • Polyurethane (optional)

Steps

  1. Set the leg angle. Lay a 2×4 flat. At each end, mark a line 1 inch from the edge at a 15° angle from perpendicular. Cut both legs to 36" and cut the ends at 15°.
  2. Assemble the A-frame. Stand the two angled legs together at the top. The tops should be flush. Clamp them. Drive two screws through the top into both legs.
  3. Add the cross brace. Position the cross brace 8" up from the bottom of the legs. Screw through both legs into the brace from the outside. This keeps the legs from spreading.
  4. Add side blocks. Cut two 2×4 blocks to fit between the legs near the top. Screw through each leg into the block. This adds side-to-side stability.
  5. Mount the top beam. Place the 28" top beam across the legs at the top. Screw up through the legs into the beam. Repeat for second sawhorse.
  6. Finish. Sand rough spots. Apply a coat of polyurethane if these will live in a garage. Store flat when not in use.
Tools: Circular saw (or miter saw), drill, clamps, speed square

#1

Floor-to-Ceiling Shelving Unit

Intermediate

The single highest-impact project for a garage. Plywood and 2×4s, under $150, delivers 30-40 feet of storage in a 6-8 foot footprint. Everything goes up — the floor clears for the car.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 2× 2×4 × 84" — Side posts (full height)
  • 8× 2×4 × 72" — Shelf boards (4 per side)
  • 2× 2×4 × 76" — Top rail + bottom rail
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 48" × 96" — Back panel
  • 4× ¾" plywood × 48" × 24" — Shelf surfaces
Materials
  • 1¼" pocket hole screws (80+)
  • 3" deck screws (16, for wall anchors)
  • Wood glue
  • Wall anchors or lag bolts (4)
  • Polyurethane (for garage conditions)

Steps

  1. Cut side posts. Two 2×4s at 84" tall (7 feet). This leaves room for a top rail and bottom rail while fitting under most garage ceilings.
  2. Build one side frame. Lay two posts parallel. Attach shelf boards between them at 20" intervals (measure from bottom). Pocket holes from the outside of posts into each shelf board.
  3. Repeat for second side. Make an identical frame. Check both are the same height — measure diagonals. They must match for the unit to sit level.
  4. Connect sides with rails. Top rail and bottom rail go across both sides. Pocket holes through the posts into the rails. This turns two frames into one unit.
  5. Add plywood shelf surfaces. Cut four 24" wide plywood panels. Screw through each shelf board (from above) into the plywood. Space them to match your shelf boards.
  6. Add back panel. Screw ¾" plywood to the back of the unit. This prevents racking (side-to-side wobble). Drive screws every 12".
  7. Move into place and anchor. Slide the unit against the wall. Anchor into wall studs with 3" deck screws through the back posts. Shim if the floor is uneven.
Tools: Table saw or circular saw, drill, pocket hole jig, level, 4ft+ clamps, stud finder
#2

Fold-Down Workbench

Beginner

A shelf that folds down into a workbench when you need it, folds flat when you don't. Reddit's #1 viral garage project. Simple hinges and 2×4s — built in a weekend, changes everything.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 24" × 48" — Workbench surface
  • 2× 2×4 × 48" — Side supports (when folded down)
  • 2× 2×4 × 20" — Mounting blocks (for wall studs)
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 24" × 36" — Wall storage panel (optional)
Materials
  • 2× 270° continuous hinges (heavy duty)
  • 3" structural screws (16)
  • Wood glue
  • Support arm with gas strut (or chain + hook)
  • Wall anchors or lag bolts (4)

Steps

  1. Cut the workbench surface. Rip plywood to 24" wide. Length depends on your wall — 48" is a good starting point. Sand edges smooth.
  2. Attach hinges to the bench. Screw one side of each continuous hinge to the edge of the workbench. Position them 8" from each end. These are the hinges that let it fold.
  3. Find and mark wall studs. Two studs spaced 16" apart. The mounting blocks attach here. Mark stud centers with a pencil.
  4. Mount the blocks to the wall. Screw 2×4 blocks into the two studs. Position them at the height where the bench folds down to work level (about 36" from floor).
  5. Attach hinges to wall blocks. Screw the other side of each hinge to the wall-mounted blocks. The bench should fold flat against the wall when done.
  6. Add a support arm. Install a gas strut, chain, or simple wooden prop that holds the bench at working angle. Without it, the bench will sag under load.
  7. Test the fold. Lift the bench — it should go down smoothly and lock at 90°. Fold up — it should lay flat. Adjust the support arm tension if needed.
Tools: Circular saw, drill, level, 4ft+ clamps, speed square
#3

Pegboard Tool Wall

Beginner

One weekend. Transforms a blank wall into an organized tool display. The easiest project on this list with outsized impact on daily usability. Pegboard goes on, tools go where you need them.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 1× ¼" pegboard × 48" × 96" (cut to desired size)
  • 4× 1×4 × 48" — Framing boards
  • 2× 1×4 × 24" — Side frame pieces
Materials
  • 1½" wood screws (20+)
  • Pegboard hooks (assorted set)
  • Wood glue
  • Paint or stain (for the frame)
  • Wall anchors or lag bolts (4)

Steps

  1. Cut pegboard to size. Measure your wall space. Cut the pegboard panel with a jigsaw or circular saw. Score and snap for cleaner cuts.
  2. Build the frame. Assemble a rectangular frame from 1×4s that's slightly larger than the pegboard (the frame will cover the raw edges). Glue + screw the corners.
  3. Paint or stain the frame. Do this before attaching the pegboard. This is where you personalize the look — dark stain for a workshop vibe, white paint for a garage look.
  4. Attach pegboard to frame. Lay pegboard face-down. Place frame on top. Screw through the frame into the pegboard from the back. Space screws every 6".
  5. Mount to wall. Hold the frame against the wall at a comfortable height. Find studs. Screw through the back of the frame into the wall studs.
  6. Arrange your tools. This is the fun part. Hang wrenches, pliers, screwdrivers, tape measure. Group by function. Everything should be reachable without moving anything else.
Tools: Jigsaw or circular saw, drill, level, speed square
#4

Corner Storage Cabinet

Intermediate

Uses dead corner space that's usually wasted. Plywood and pocket holes create enclosed storage for cans, bins, and bulk items. Covers the "clutter that doesn't belong on display" problem.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 2× ¾" plywood × 24" × 36" — Side panels (angled)
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 24" × 36" — Back panel
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 24" × 24" — Top panel
  • 2× ¾" plywood × 24" × 30" — Doors (cut with miter)
  • 1× ¾" plywood × 24" × 24" — Interior shelf
Materials
  • 1¼" pocket hole screws (40+)
  • Wood glue
  • 2× cabinet door hinges (pair)
  • 2× magnetic door catches
  • 1¾" wood screws (4, for wall)

Steps

  1. Cut side panels to angle. Each side panel is a right triangle. Cut the angled edge at 45° — the two sides meet at 90° in the corner.
  2. Build the box. Attach the back panel to the sides using pocket holes. Then attach the top panel. You now have an open box shape with two angled sides and a back.
  3. Add the interior shelf. Cut shelf to fit inside the box. Pocket hole screws from the sides into the shelf. Position at desired height (typically waist level).
  4. Build and hang the doors. Cut doors to cover the front opening. Attach hinges to the doors, then mount to the cabinet frame. Install magnetic catches to keep doors closed.
  5. Finish. Sand all surfaces. Apply paint or stain. This is a garage cabinet — a coat of semi-gloss enamel makes it easy to wipe clean.
  6. Anchor to wall. Screw through the back panel into wall studs. Corner cabinets can tip forward if loaded heavily — anchoring is essential.
Tools: Table saw, drill, pocket hole jig, clamps, sander, circular saw (for angled cuts)
#5

Bike & Long-Tool Wall Rack

Beginner

Under $20 in materials. A wall-mounted rack for bikes, rakes, shovels, and hoses clears a huge amount of floor space. One-hour build. Family Handyman's most-shared garage project.

Build Plan

Cut List
  • 1× 2×4 × 72" — Main horizontal rail (cut to wall length)
  • 4× 2×4 × 8" — Support blocks (2 per hook position)
  • 8× 2×4 × 4" — Hook arms (angled cuts)
Materials
  • 2½" wood screws (20+)
  • 3" structural screws (4, into studs)
  • Wood glue
  • 4× heavy-duty wall anchors or lag bolts
  • Paint or stain (optional)

Steps

  1. Cut the main rail. Cut the 2×4 to fit your wall space. Measure from stud center to stud center. Position hooks every 12-16" apart.
  2. Cut the hook arms. Cut four 4" pieces at a 45° angle on one end. These are the V-notches that hold bike tires or tool handles. The angle is critical — without it, bikes will slide off.
  3. Assemble hooks to rail. Glue and screw each hook arm to the bottom of the rail at your marked positions. The angled end faces outward.
  4. Add support blocks. Cut 2×4 blocks to fit between the rail and wall. Screw through the rail into the blocks. These prevent the rail from sagging under weight.
  5. Mount to wall. Position the rail at a comfortable height (top of bike tire should be below shoulder level). Screw through the rail into wall studs. Use lag bolts if studs are spaced 24" apart.
  6. Load it up. Hang bikes first (heaviest items). Then rakes, shovels, hoses. Leave empty hooks for future tools. This is the last step of any garage project.
Tools: Circular saw or miter saw, drill, level, speed square
